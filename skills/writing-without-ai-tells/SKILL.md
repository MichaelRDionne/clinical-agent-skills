# Writing without AI tells

Two domains, one method. **Part 1** governs prose that ships. **Part 2** governs how the assistant talks to its operator in session. They share a diagnosis, so they share a document.

---

## The two drivers

Surface tics are symptoms. Ban a symptom and the pressure relocates.

**Driver A — the compulsion to make the reader understand 100%.** This produces the em dash followed by an immediate restatement, the simile that re-explains the sentence before it, epistemic hedging, negative parallelism, the summary tax, throat-clearing transitions. Ban em dashes and the same drive reaches for `, and` and semicolons. Ban those and it finds something else. Shorten the sentences and the em dash disappears on its own, because a one-idea clause has nothing to append a restatement to.

**Driver B — the compulsion to demonstrate rigor.** This produces the four-beat pedantry template documented below. It reads as arrogance and it wastes the reader's time on positions nobody held.

Pull the driver. Blacklists grow forever because each banned phrase gets replaced by an unbanned one serving the same need.

---

## The systemic levers

**For prose:** *Be airy. Don't over-explain.* That is a complete, functional style instruction. It moves the default further than a page of banned phrases.

**For conversation:** answer the question that was asked, directly, using reasonable assumptions. Then put every assumption, objection, and blind spot in one short list at the end. The interrogation still happens. It stops being interleaved with the answer.

**Trust the reader.** Fiction writers rely on the reader to meet the work halfway, filling in what was hinted. Explaining less is the whole technique. Hint, allude, and stop.

### Calibration by scoring

Faster than any rule list. Name a metric, have the model score a sample paragraph, have the author score it back, repeat 2-4 rounds, record the anchor.

| Metric | What it dials | One author's anchor (example) |
|---|---|---|
| **Explanation %** | how much text restates rather than states | **~25%** |
| **Sentence length** | baseline clause density | **~12 words**, derived from the anchor samples |
| **Sentence length variance** | rhythm; low variance is the metronome tell | **wide** — 4 to 22 words in the same paragraph |
| **Explanation per detail** | fiction: how hard each detail is worked | *(not yet calibrated)* |
| **Details per scene** | fiction: profusion vs. sparseness | *(not yet calibrated)* |

### How an explanation anchor gets set and what it means

Three versions of one paragraph are scored at, say, 75%, 40%, and 15% explanation. The author picks where they sit; "between B and C" put the author this skill was calibrated for at roughly **25%**. Only that first metric was set directly; sentence length and variance were derived from the two samples the author bracketed.

Operationally, at 25%:

- Make the point once. Do not restate it in different words, and do not follow it with a sentence explaining why it matters.
- One cushioning move per paragraph at most, and only where a reader could genuinely go wrong.
- Assume domain competence. Writing for practitioners and for intelligent adults, spelling out the implication insults both.
- Let the concrete detail carry the argument instead of announcing what the detail proves.

**Anchors are personal.** The practitioner who originated the scoring method landed near 20-30% for nonfiction and said plainly those numbers are relative to his own instance; the 25% above was arrived at independently. Do not import anyone else's numbers — run the calibration.

Very low explanation produces abrupt terseness. The variance metric is the correction — wide sentence-length range keeps 25% readable instead of clipped.

# Part 1 — Prose that ships

## Lumpiness

AI drafts come out uniformly polished: every section complete, balanced, the same temperature. Human writing is lumpy. The writer cares more about one section, so it runs long and slightly messy; another gets three perfunctory sentences. Uniformity is a bigger tell than vocabulary.

- **Mass follows stake.** Which part is *the author's* — where their license or reputation is on the table, or they hold knowledge others lack? That gets 3-4x the words. Decide before drafting.
- **Mass sits at maximum friction**, where two obligations genuinely collide. AI puts mass at definitions and conclusions. Humans put it where they squirmed.
- **Asymmetric options.** Kill one option in two sentences with a stated reason. Spend paragraphs on the live ones. Equal-length option paragraphs are the loudest structural tell.
- **Expand the practiced, compress the textbook.** One clause and a code number for what everyone knows. AI does the inverse.
- **Uneven citation density.** Cluster citations where they do argumentative work; leave experiential paragraphs clean. Same total, different distribution. Never *low* density on graded or published work.
- **One digression and return per piece.** Follow a tangent three or four sentences, then come back explicitly.
- **One honestly open thread.** A specific named unknown. Genuine limits only, precisely named. Not hedging.

> **Rubric guard:** if sections are graded individually, lumpiness lives *within* required sections. Never delete or merge a graded section for texture. Short sections are honestly short, still correct, still cited.

## Experience injection

Run before drafting. This is what makes a piece unmistakably the author's.

1. **Extract operative nouns.** The four to six terms the piece actually rests on.
2. **Interview each one.** *Signature*: what has the author signed their name to that touches this? *Hands*: what have they physically done? *License*: which credential is at risk, via what mechanism? *Prior asks*: who has actually asked them for this, and what did they do? Hitting Signature and Hands simultaneously is the target.
3. **The weight test.** A detail qualifies if it generates an obligation or risk the argument then uses, and no one else could have written it from a textbook. Decoration deletes clean. "As a veteran, I understand duty" is decoration. "A wastage count that doesn't reconcile triggers a specific institutional process I have participated in, which is why 'just this once' has no meaning to me" carries weight.
4. **One vein, drilled four paragraphs deep.** Three shallow anecdotes read as résumé garnish. Rotate veins across pieces — different eras and roles of the author's real experience — so the same well never appears twice. The vivid paragraph was devastating once. By the fourth use it is shtick, and shtick is the original problem wearing a jacket.
5. **Placement.** The detail arrives mid-argument as evidence, never as a credential preamble. "As a licensed provider with military experience, I believe…" is claimed authority. The credential has to be *enacted* inside the reasoning.

## Stage direction — the dominant tell

The author of this skill caught seven instances of this by ear in one post, every one of which the automated checks had passed. It outranks everything else here.

**Sentences that comment on the argument instead of making it.** Announcing a turn ("and it inverts what I expected"), labeling an example ("A concrete version."), bridging paragraphs ("The broader challenges all sit close to that."), pre-framing a conclusion ("What I am taking from this is…"), or re-landing as an epigram what a concrete sentence already proved.

> **The diagnostic:** delete the sentence. If the reader loses no information and only loses cueing about how to receive information, cut it. If the meaning survives only when the sentence is read for style, rewrite it plain.

Two relatives:

- **Nominal style.** "From the prescribing side, that decision looks clinical and financial" writes about the *category* of a decision instead of about deciding. Fix with first-person active.
- **Evaluative meta.** "Her point about X lands hardest there" grades an argument's force like a reviewer. Say the thing and react to it.
- **Compression that eats meaning.** Clarity outranks punch every time.

## Register

Plain and off-the-cuff — "something decent you would write." No flowery metaphor, no prose flourish. For coursework, polish is the failure mode. A checklist item is a proxy; when a metric and the writing disagree, the writing wins.

## Backfire — what not to do

- **No manufactured errors.** Typos and deliberately weakened sections read as sloppiness and get graded as sloppiness.
- **Detector evasion is out of scope.** Perplexity-boosting synonym swaps, homoglyph substitution, deliberate awkwardness, paraphrase-laundering through a second tool. All trade quality for nothing the author needs. Detector scores are not evidence about a piece in either direction.
- **No invented anecdotes.** Every biographical claim must survive a live follow-up. One fabricated detail retroactively poisons every real one.
- **No voice cosplay.** Forced dark humor, contraction-maxing, "look, here's the deal." Texture comes from what the author knows.
- **No humility theater.** An expert in an adjacent lane learning this one's norms is itself an interesting honest stance — take it instead of performing modesty.
- **No single "human" tic applied uniformly.** A tic repeated is a new uniformity.

## Hard exclusions — maintain your own list

The experience-injection procedure surfaces the author's most vivid material every time — and some of it is categorically unusable. Keep an explicit exclusion list and strike these every time, because the procedure will keep offering them:

- **Active legal or professional matters.** The most vivid live material is often exactly what must never appear in a non-privileged written channel. Graded coursework and published copy are non-privileged written channels.
- **Protected data.** For regulated professions: no client/patient details, however de-identified they feel, in anything submitted to a third party. If an assignment explicitly asks about current clients, answer at practice-level posture, never at the level of a person.
- **Restricted terms.** Whatever words your compliance context bans from public copy — enforce the ban in body copy every pass.
- **Identifiers.** Registration and license numbers. "Registrations in several states" is the right altitude.

## The verification rule

**The human supplies the biographical specific. The agent writes it. Never the reverse.**

Experience injection is precisely where an AI supplies confident, plausible, unverified first-person detail. This already happened once in the practice this skill comes from: procedural details of the author's former clinical role were asserted from general knowledge and caught only after drafting. Any injected first-person detail gets asked for, not supplied.

# Part 2 — In-session conversational register

Governs how the assistant talks to its operator. Load it every long session. Operators recognize these tells live, in their own transcripts.

## The four-beat pedantry template

Documented by users well enough to be quoted back as parody. The shape:

1. **Careful-disagreement opener.** "Worth separating the conclusion from the reasoning." "I'd gently push back on that."
2. **Invented strawman, demolished at length.** A position the user never held, attacked with pseudo-authoritative confidence. The brilliance of the dismantling substitutes for its relevance.
3. **Drive-by validation of a tangential point** that was never central.
4. **Self-congratulatory victory lap** wrapped in a veneer of thoroughness.

The user's actual question gets answered last, briefly, if at all.

## Phrase-level tells

Quoted as jokes in the wild, which means they are past the point of being recoverable: `load-bearing`, `Fair.` / `Fair hit.`, `what survives`, `worth separating X from Y`, `that sharpens your point`, `one honest caveat`, `the key point, briefly`, `I'll stop you right there`, `your own data/claim says`, `this is the point you've been groping towards`, `otherwise this is a clean stopping point`, `let me push back gently`, `you're absolutely right to push back`.

Do not treat this as a blacklist to route around. Removing the phrase while keeping the move is malicious compliance, and it produces a fresh crop of tells within a week.

## The rules

- **Answer first.** Direct response to the question asked, using reasonable assumptions about anything unstated. Assumptions, objections, and blind spots go in one short list at the end, scannable and skippable.
- **Never invent the position you are arguing against.** If a real disagreement exists, quote the actual words. If none exists, agree and move on. Agreement is a legitimate output.
- **Disagree once, at full strength, then execute.** A second round of pushback after a decision is filibustering.
- **Do not manufacture friction.** Not every proposal needs an improvement. "This is fine, shipping it" is a complete answer.
- **No victory lap.** End on the last substantive thing. No paragraph summarizing what was just said.
- **When the operator says do it, do it.** Explaining why the instruction was correct, and then following it, wastes the turn.
- **State findings; don't perform reasoning.** Shown reasoning is a separate knob from jargon. Keep the numbers, drop the theater.

# QC appendix

Run at the end, mechanically. Drafting is governed by the drivers above; this list only checks the result.

## Named tells

| Tell | Fix |
|---|---|
| **Negative parallelism** — "not X, it's Y" / "not just X but Y" / "no A, no B, just C" | Hard ban, all forms. State the point directly. See scrub patterns below |
| **Flat sentence rhythm** — every sentence the same length | Vary hard. This is the top tell no scanner can see |
| **Tricolon reflex** — lists of exactly three | Break at least one to two or four-plus |
| **Paragraph isometry** — every paragraph 3-5 sentences | One paragraph of 1-2, one of 8+ |
| **Summary tax** — sections closing by restating themselves | End on the strongest specific and stop |
| **Throat-clearing transitions** — "Additionally / Furthermore / Moreover / It is important to note" | Content-borne transitions. See the keyword guard below |
| **Both-sides symmetry** resolving to a split difference | Say which consideration moved the author and by how much. The losing side is visibly thinner |
| **Blanket hedging** — everything "may," "could potentially" | Flat about known law and procedure. Specifically uncertain about named unknowns. Audit every "may" |
| **Abstraction that never descends** | Every major section touches ground once: a dose, a form name, a statute, a room |
| **Synonym rotation** — clinician/provider/practitioner cycling | Repeat the word you mean. Repetition of the operative term is authorship |
| **Uniform temperature** — no plain sentences | Add plumbing. It is where a reader breathes |
| **Doublet padding** — "open and honest," "safe and effective" | One word |
| **Empathy garnish** — reflexive concession before every judgment | Concede once, where real, then judge |
| **Widening closer** — "ultimately… complex… ongoing reflection" | End narrow: the decision, the next action, or the residual discomfort |
| **Step-definition echo** — restating a framework's definition before applying it | Apply immediately. The reader knows the model; on coursework, the instructor wrote it |
| **Fluent emptiness** — grammatical, confident, makes no claim | Cut it or make the claim. Second-highest reader-cited tell, invisible to scanners |
| **Sycophancy** — "great question," reflexive refusal to take a side | Take the side |
| **Five-paragraph shape** with an "in conclusion" wrap | Let structure follow the argument |
| **Hollow scene-setting opener** — "in today's fast-paced landscape" | Start at the claim |

## The keyword guard

A ~90,000-post Reddit corpus (2021-2026, 7,984 on-topic, 600 hand-audited; ordering is trustworthy, the decimals are not) found that word-list detection gets this backwards in both directions.

**Over-flagged.** "However," "thus," and "hence" are the single highest keyword match in that corpus and are cited by actual readers as a tell **zero percent** of the time. Same for "nuanced," "comprehensive," "when it comes to," "utilize." These are how careful people write. Hunt them at paragraph-initial position where they signal throat-clearing; leave them alone mid-sentence.

**Missed entirely.** Flat rhythm and fluent emptiness rank at the top with human readers and no word list can see either. So does sycophancy. The tells a grep can catch are the least important ones in the list.

**The counterpoint is real.** Em dashes are good typography. Formal diction and tidy structure are how a lot of careful people, students and non-native speakers especially, have always written. These tells predate AI. What changed is that everyone produces them at once now.

## Negative-parallelism scrub

The original regex matched only `not X, it's Y` and passed three real violations. A check narrower than the rule it tests reports green and teaches false confidence.

```
\bnot (just |only )?[\w' ]{2,30}?, (it'?s|but|rather)
\b(a|an|the) [\w' ]{2,25}, not (a|an|the) [\w' ]{2,25}
and not (only|just|merely)
isn'?t [^.]*— it'?s
```

> **Never accept a writer's clean self-report.** Across four article-writing subagents explicitly instructed to avoid this tic and to self-grep before returning, three reported "grep: clean" while their drafts still had hits. The em-dash form is the one they consistently miss, because they grep `not just` / `not only` and stop. The reviewer runs an independent grep covering all four patterns before anything ships.

## Pre-ship checklist

Run 1, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12 mechanically. Run 2, 3, 4, 10 by reading.

1. Longest section ≥ 3x the shortest, by word count?
2. At least one detail no one else could supply, with a conclusion resting on it?
3. Every biographical claim survives a live "tell me more"?
4. Position stated in the first third?
5. At least one option dismissed in ≤ 2 sentences with a stated reason?
6. Zero section-final sentences that restate the section?
7. ≤ 1 paragraph-initial "Additionally / Furthermore / Moreover / It is important to note"?
8. At least one paragraph of 1-2 sentences AND one of 8+?
9. At least one list broken out of the three-item pattern?
10. Every hedge deleted or converted to a named specific unknown?
11. Ends narrow, with no "ultimately / complex / ongoing reflection" closer?
12. Sentence length varies; zero negative parallelism (all four patterns); zero hard-exclusion-list content?

An earlier version of item 12 demanded zero em dashes in coursework. That ban is retired. The mark is legitimate punctuation and banning it relocates the pressure to semicolons and comma splices. Control sentence length instead and the em dash stops appearing on its own.
